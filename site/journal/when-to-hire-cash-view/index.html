When to hire, from a cash point of view

A hire is usually the largest recurring commitment a small business makes. Here is how to check it against your cash before you sign the offer letter.

Nadia BrandtCo-founder and CEO

In this article

Most hiring decisions are made on workload: the team is stretched, so you hire. That is the right reason. The cash question is different, and it is worth asking separately: can the business carry this person through its worst month, before they have made any difference?

Start with the real cost

Salary is only part of it. Payroll taxes, benefits, equipment and software add between 15% and 30% in the US, depending on the role and the state.

That is about $7,450 a month, or roughly $1,720 a week. Use that number, not the salary, in every check below.

Allow for the ramp

New people take time to pay for themselves. A salesperson may take two or three quarters to bring in steady revenue; a production hire may be productive in a month. Plan for the cost from day one and the benefit from a realistic later date.

Plan for the cost from day one, and for the benefit from a realistic, later date.

The trough test

Add the fully loaded cost to your forecast from the start date, with no extra revenue. Then look at the lowest week in the next twelve months.

  • Still above your cash floor: you can afford the hire, even if it takes longer than planned to pay off.
  • Below the floor, above zero: it can work, but decide now what you will do in that week. A later start date, a deposit from a client, or an undrawn credit line.
  • Below zero: not yet. Look again after the next busy season, or consider part-time or contract help first.

Time the start date

For seasonal businesses, the start date can matter as much as the decision. Hiring just before the busy season means the person is trained when you need them and their first months are paid for by peak income. Hiring at the start of the quiet months does the opposite.

Before you send the offer

Run three versions: the hire as planned, the hire with revenue arriving three months late, and the hire with your largest client paying 45 days late. If all three stay above zero, you are making a workload decision, not a cash gamble.
