How to set a cash floor

A cash floor is the balance you agree never to go below. Setting one turns a vague worry into a number you can plan around.

Nadia BrandtCo-founder and CEO
Published
August 26, 2026
Reading time
3 minutes

What a floor has to coverA worked exampleUsing the floor

Most owners carry a number in their head below which they start to feel uneasy. A cash floor is that number, written down and worked out properly, so the forecast can warn you before you reach it rather than after.

It is not the same as zero. Zero is when you cannot pay someone. The floor is the point where you still have time and options.

What a floor has to cover

A good floor answers one question: if no money came in for a while, how long could the business keep its promises? That means three things.

  • Fixed costs for a set number of weeks. Payroll, rent, insurance, loan repayments and the software you cannot switch off.
  • Money that is not yours. Sales tax collected, payroll taxes withheld, customer deposits for work not yet done.
  • One bad surprise. A late client, a broken machine, a supplier asking for payment up front.

A worked example

Take a 14-person studio. Payroll is $38,200 every two weeks, rent is $6,450 a month and other fixed costs come to about $12,000 a month. That is roughly $101,000 of fixed costs a month, or about $23,500 a week.

Four weeks is a reasonable starting point for a business with steady income. If your income is lumpy or seasonal, use six to eight weeks. If you sell to large clients on long terms, size the surprise around your biggest one.

Using the floor

Once the floor exists, the forecast has a line to measure against. Any week that dips below it is a week you know about in advance, which means you can move a payment, chase an invoice or arrange finance while it is still cheap and calm to do so.

It also works the other way. Cash comfortably above the floor for the whole forecast is cash you can use: for stock bought at a better price, for a hire, or for paying down a loan early.

Review it twice a year

Fixed costs creep. Revisit the floor whenever payroll changes by more than about 10%, when you sign a new lease, or at least every six months.
